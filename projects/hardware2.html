Hackathon Hardware Sign Out System

April 2020 - Present

View My Prototype

Desktop

Background

I had the honor of being the lead director of MakeUofT 2020, a hardware hackathon (“makeathon”) at the University of Toronto. We hosted 300 students over a weekend and provided these hackers with 5000+ items of hardware, as well as mentors and workshops to help them use it. After working with MakeUofT for the last 2 years, I saw the inefficient process we distributed hardware, and I wanted to fix it.


The Problem

These were the initial issues we noticed with the hardware signout process:

  • Up to 2+ hour wait during the initial rush
  • Participants making unreasonable requests
    • “I need 5 webcams!”
  • Unable to update the database on the fly on the standard hackathon system we were using

After I conducted research with both the hackers and tech team members, I gained a lot of insight on the specifics causing the above pain points.


Research

I conducted one-on-one interviews with each person and asked them:

  1. Describe your experience signing out and picking up hardware the day-of
  2. Were there any steps in the process you felt were unnecessary?
  3. Were there any steps you felt were missing?
  4. Did this experience bring a positive or negative experience for you?
  5. How has this experience impacted your overall experience at MakeUofT?

From this, I sorted the issues and comments my interviewees brought up, split by the hacker experience and tech team experience.


Hacker Side

  • Long wait times cut into limited hacking time
  • Splits up team as one waits in line and the others brainstorm
  • Less inclined to listen to the opening ceremonies - want to get into line ASAP
  • False sense of certainty that they'll get all they ask for

Tech Team Side

  • Teams requesting more hardware than they actually need
  • Need to add new components to the database on the fly
  • System allowed individuals to sign out hardware - made returns harder and longer
  • Needed to track broken and lost items

With this, I generated a user persona for each stakeholder.

Goal

Shorten the time of the initial hardware rush as much as possible while still providing teams with a reasonable amount of hardware.


Outcome

Time was the most valuable to all parties. I knew that the underlying issue was with our hardware flow. No amount of additional technical tools and resources could speed up the process if everyone ordered right as hacking began. Step one was to redesign our hardware pickup flow.


My first idea was what I called the “Fast Food Flow”. It feels like the experience one has at McDonald’s. They go up to the cashier, the cashier enters info onto their interface, and once the order is complete in the back, the customer picks it up.

After consulting with both the tech team and the web development team, the following pros and cons were decided upon


Pros

  • Encourages hackers to arrive earlier due to first-come-first-serve system
  • Tech team becomes the “middle-man” to reject outlandish orders before they’re made
  • More time for tech team to package orders
  • Simpler code architecture

Cons

  • Rejecting outlandish orders is due to human error and subjectiveness
  • Hacker has no front-end to view their checked out items

My second, and final, idea was what I called the “eCommerceFlow”. Both sides have a front end. The hacker is able to view inventory on the site and add to their cart. The tech team then views orders, and is able to reject and accept parts of orders.


Pros

  • 12AM drop sets an even playing field
  • Hard-coded restraints to prevent hogging hardware and outlandish requests

Cons

  • Complex code architecture

Ultimately, it was the web team that pushed for the eCommerce Flow. Despite the fact that there was a lot more work for them, they wanted to put in the work at the beginning so we could use the product for years to come.


The final design aimed to alleviate all the issues that we faced in the last 2 years. The hacker flow was aimed to give participants sufficient time to check out a fair amount of hardware. The admin side aimed to condense all the tasks our team had to do onto one page so that more time could be spent packaging hardware.

I decided to make my wireframes the final product! It made sense as we planned on making the site open source, and branding is very specific and important for hackathons. The site was designed with Material-UI elements, as we used React to develop the front-end and Django template for the backend. This allowed us to set variables that those forking our website could alter. Sass was used to control colors, fonts, and sizes. Material UI allows for even finer design details, such as button styles, shadows, and much more.
